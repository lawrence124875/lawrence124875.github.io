After casting a hexagram, many beginners get stuck: what do these six lines actually mean? This guide doesn't explain any individual hexagram. Instead it shows **how a hexagram is built**, so you know where to start reading.

## Start with a single line

A line (yao) is the smallest unit of a hexagram. There are only two kinds:

- **Yang line** ⚊: solid
- **Yin line** ⚋: broken in the middle

## Three lines: the eight trigrams

Stack three lines and you get 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 combinations: the eight trigrams. They're named Qian, Dui, Li, Zhen, Xun, Kan, Gen and Kun, and stand for heaven, lake, fire, thunder, wind, water, mountain and earth.

## Six lines: the 64 hexagrams

Put one trigram on top of another and you get one of the 64 hexagrams:

- The bottom three lines are the **lower (inner) trigram**, often read as yourself, your inner state, or how things begin.
- The top three lines are the **upper (outer) trigram**, often read as your surroundings, other people, or how things develop.

The six lines are numbered **from the bottom up**, one to six. Position carries meaning too: the bottom line is like the beginning of a situation, and the top line is like its end.

## Primary hexagram, changing lines, relating hexagram

- **Primary hexagram**: the hexagram you cast, showing the current situation.
- **Changing lines**: lines that "move" when you cast (6s and 9s with coins, or the line you calculate with Plum Blossom). They mark what is shifting, and they deserve the most attention.
- **Relating hexagram** (or changed hexagram): what you get when you flip the changing lines. It shows the direction of change.

Think of it as a story: **the primary hexagram is now, the changing lines are the turning points, and the relating hexagram is where things may be heading.**

## How to read different numbers of changing lines

Traditions vary. Here is one common, simplified approach that suits beginners:

| Changing lines | Focus on |
|---|---|
| 0 | The primary hexagram as a whole |
| 1 | That line first, with the primary hexagram as context |
| 2–3 | Those lines, comparing the primary and relating hexagrams |
| 4–5 | Shift your focus to the relating hexagram |
| 6 | The relating hexagram as a whole |

Teachers' rules differ a little. Pick a system that makes sense to you and use it consistently.

## Going further: the nuclear hexagram

Some readers also look at the **nuclear hexagram**. Take lines 2–4 of the primary hexagram as the lower trigram and lines 3–5 as the upper trigram. The new hexagram is used to look at what's hidden inside the situation. Beginners can skip this until the primary and relating hexagrams feel familiar.

## Summary

A simple reading order: first look at how the two trigrams combine and get a feel for the overall picture. Then note where the changing lines sit. Finally, look at the relating hexagram and consider where things could go. The aim isn't to memorise answers. It's to use this structure to organise your own thoughts.
