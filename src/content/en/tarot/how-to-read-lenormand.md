A Lenormand deck has only 36 cards, each showing one simple, everyday object. Its defining feature: **single cards are simple and direct, and the real information comes from how cards combine**. If you've learned tarot, Lenormand asks you to switch to a different way of thinking.

## How Lenormand differs from tarot

| | Tarot | Lenormand |
|---|---|---|
| Single card | Rich imagery, many layers | Simple, direct meaning |
| Reading focus | Each card's symbolism | How cards combine |
| Reversals | Some readers use them | Usually not used |
| Style | Inner and psychological | Concrete, practical events |

## The core idea: read cards like sentences

A common approach for two cards:

- **The first card** is the noun: what the topic is.
- **The second card** is the adjective: what that topic is like.

Just as "a red car" differs from "the car's redness", **card order changes the meaning**. When practising, say each pair out loud as a phrase and check whether it makes sense.

## From two cards to three

Once pairs feel natural, practise three-card "sentences":

1. Read cards 1 + 2 to get one phrase.
2. Read cards 2 + 3 to get another.
3. Join the two into one complete sentence.

The middle card is usually the centre of the sentence.

## The 9-card box

Lay 9 cards in a 3 × 3 grid:

- **The centre card** is the heart of the matter.
- **The four corners** give background.
- **Rows, columns and diagonals** can each be read as a sentence.

The 9-card box is a good bridge from three-card lines to the big spread.

## The Grand Tableau

The Grand Tableau lays out all 36 cards, usually as 8 × 4 + 4 or 9 × 4. Readers look at:

- **Where your significator card falls** and which cards surround it
- **Distance** between cards: the closer, the more direct the influence
- **Houses:** each position also carries the theme of one card

It's a lot of information, so try it after pairs, lines of three and the 9-card box feel comfortable.

## How to practise

1. **Draw two cards a day**, describe the pair in one sentence and write it down.
2. **Stick to one set of rules**, such as "the first card is always the topic".
3. **Note everyday events** and see which combinations show up in which situations.
4. **Don't rush to memorise combination tables.** Build your own sentences first, then compare with references.

Lenormand's charm is that it's simple, direct and close to daily life. Practise combinations and you'll find 36 cards can tell more stories than you'd expect.
