"How many English words do I really need?" There's no single answer, but there is a very useful way to think about it: **the 80/20 rule**.

## What is the 80/20 rule?

The 80/20 rule, or Pareto principle, began as an observation in economics: about 20% of causes produce about 80% of results. The pattern shows up in many places, and language is no exception.

## How uneven is English vocabulary?

Linguists have found that how often a word appears is roughly inversely proportional to its rank. This is called **Zipf's law**. Put simply:

- The most common word appears about twice as often as the second
- The top few dozen words make up a large share of any text
- Most words are actually rare

In other words, **a small set of common words carries most of English**. That's the 80/20 rule applied to vocabulary.

## How much can you understand with different vocabulary sizes?

Figures vary with the type of text and how words are counted. These are rough ranges from commonly cited research:

| Vocabulary | Approximate coverage of general text | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| About 1,000 word families | About 70–80% | Follow the gist of simple conversations |
| About 2,000–3,000 words | About 85–90% | Handle daily life and simple reading |
| About 8,000–9,000 word families | About 98% | Read novels and newspapers fairly comfortably |

A "word family" is a word plus its related forms. For example, *use*, *used* and *useful* count as one family.

## What does this mean for learning vocabulary?

### 1. Learn the most frequent words first

The first 2,000–3,000 words give the best return on your time. A frequency-ranked list such as the NGSL is a great starting point. (See also: [What is the NGSL?](/en/english/what-is-ngsl/))

### 2. Learn common words deeply

Common words appear often and have many uses. Rather than adding a few hundred rare words, learn the pronunciation, collocations and example sentences of common words well.

### 3. Learn rare words through context

Beyond the core, the number of words explodes, but each one appears rarely. At that point, the most effective approach is **lots of reading and listening**, so you meet words in context and remember them naturally.

## A practical learning order

1. Build a base with a core word list, aiming to recognise words when you hear or see them.
2. Use example sentences and audio to turn common words into words you can use.
3. Extend toward your goal: exam vocabulary, work vocabulary or your hobbies.
4. Keep reading and listening so the remaining words build up in context.

Learning vocabulary isn't about the biggest number. It's about whether you've truly learned the common words. Get that 20% right and you'll understand most of the English you meet.
