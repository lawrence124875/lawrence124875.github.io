If you've ever wondered, "There are so many English words, so which should I learn first?", the NGSL is a great answer.

## What is the NGSL?

NGSL stands for **New General Service List**. It was published in 2013 by researchers Charles Browne, Brent Culligan and Joseph Phillips. It lists the **roughly 2,800 most frequent and useful core words in English**. The current version, 1.01, has 2,809 words.

What makes it special:

- **Built from a corpus:** based on about 273 million words of text from the Cambridge English Corpus
- **High coverage:** know these words and you'll recognise about 90% of the words in general English texts
- **Free and open:** released under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence, so anyone can download and use it

## How is the NGSL different from the GSL?

The NGSL builds on Michael West's **General Service List (GSL)** from 1953. The GSL was hugely influential, but it was based on texts from decades earlier. Some of its words are no longer common, and it lacks many words that are common today.

The NGSL recounted word frequencies using modern texts. It keeps the GSL's idea of a small core list with high coverage, while matching today's English much better.

## Why do core words matter so much?

Word use in English is very uneven: **a small number of common words make up most of any text**. Words like *the*, *be* and *have* appear in almost every sentence, while many words appear only once in a whole book.

So instead of working through a dictionary from page one, learn the 2,800 most frequent words well first. It's the approach that takes the least time for the most noticeable result. (See also: [How many English words do you need to know?](/en/english/how-many-words/))

## Who is the NGSL for?

- **Learners rebuilding a weak foundation**
- **Test takers (TOEIC, IELTS and others) who want to fill gaps in basic vocabulary**
- **Readers who keep getting stuck on unknown words**
- **Anyone who wants a clear order for learning vocabulary**

## How to use the NGSL to plan your learning

1. **Follow the frequency order.** The NGSL is ranked by how common each word is, so start at the top.
2. **Set targets in chunks**, such as 100 words at a time, and move on when you finish each one.
3. **Recognise first, then use.** First aim to understand words when you hear or see them, then practise speaking and writing them.
4. **Review regularly.** Use spaced repetition so you don't forget what you've learned.

## Related word lists

The same team has published lists for specific goals, such as the NAWL for academic English, the TSL for TOEIC and the BSL for business English. Once the NGSL is solid, you can extend in whichever direction fits your goal.
